**Raj Balakrishnan (14:01):** Purpose today is your view of the renewal book, not mine. I want the concerns, not the wins. Marcus, start.

**Marcus Delaney (14:02):** Capacity. I'll keep saying it until it changes. Eighteen maturities inside 180 days, two advisors, about seventy live files each between renewals, new originations and refis. Diane wants contact at 120 days. At seventy files, my people are triaging by whoever shouts loudest, which is almost never the client who is quietly getting quoted somewhere else.

**Raj (14:04):** What's the failure mode in practice?

**Marcus (14:04):** The high-value files get worked, which is correct. Everything under about three hundred thousand gets nothing until the automated maturity letter goes out at thirty days. That's the segment we're losing. And by the time that letter lands the client already has a number from someone else, so the letter reads like we woke up late.

**Nadia Osei (14:06):** I'd add that when we do wake up late, the only lever left is price. So capacity problems become pricing problems one month later.

**Marcus (14:07):** That's exactly it. I lost a six-forty file last month. Nobody called until day twenty-two. There was no rate we could have offered at day twenty-two that would have felt like anything other than a panic discount.

**Raj (14:08):** Sophie.

**Sophie Tremblay (14:08):** Mine isn't capacity. My advisors have time. They don't have room. The caisses and two credit unions are quoting thirty to forty basis points under our posted-less-discount rate and they're advertising it on bus shelters two blocks from my door. Clients walk in with the sheet already printed.

**Sophie (14:10):** Camille had four clients in one week ask her to match something thirty-five under our floor. Her discretion is fifteen. So the conversation ends with "let me escalate," the client leaves without a decision, and two of those four have since asked for a payout statement. That's not a pricing loss, that's a process loss. We lost them in the room.

**Raj (14:12):** Is fifteen the binding constraint, or is it the escalation time?

**Sophie (14:12):** Both, but if you made me choose — the escalation time. If I could say yes to twenty-five in the room, same day, I'd hold most of these. Five days to answer is the same as no.

**Nadia (14:14):** Agreed, and Calgary is a third thing again. It's not capacity and it's not really competitors. It's payment shock. Big chunk of my book is self-employed, originated twenty-one, twenty-two, around two percent, twenty-five year amortization. They're renewing at four-eight to five-two. Their payment goes up seventy to a hundred and thirty percent.

**Raj (14:16):** How are those conversations going?

**Nadia (14:16):** Badly, and not because of the rate. Three of Fatima's clients have said out loud they're thinking about selling. Two asked to stretch the amortization back toward thirty years, which we can do, but it triggers a full credit re-test, and self-employed income verification is taking us six to nine business days. Meanwhile a monoline will do the same thing on a straight renewal without re-verifying, because there's no new money.

**Nadia (14:18):** And here's the part that worries me most. My advisors are being asked "should I just sell?" and they're improvising an answer. We are not licensed to answer that at the counter. I need approved words and somewhere to refer the client.

**Raj (14:19):** That's a compliance action, I'll own getting it. Derek's joined — Derek, you've heard the pricing shape of this.

**Derek Fontaine (14:21):** I have. Let me put a number on the other side. We granted forty-one pricing exceptions across your three branches last quarter. Every one of them was individually defensible. Collectively they mean the grid is no longer the grid. If Montréal genuinely needs a structurally lower floor, I would rather change the floor than approve the same exception forty more times — because right now the exception volume is what an auditor will find, not any individual decision.

**Sophie (14:23):** I'll take that trade. Give me a Québec floor twenty under national and I'll stop escalating.

**Derek (14:23):** Then send me evidence, not anecdote. Ten or so competitor sheets, dated, comparable profiles. If the evidence supports twenty, I'll recommend twenty.

**Sophie (14:24):** You'll have eleven of them.

**Marcus (14:25):** Can I raise something nobody owns? Broker-originated renewals. I have six in the 180-day book, about two point four million. These clients have never met my branch. They don't return branch calls. At maturity they go back to their broker. The broker channel team tells me renewals aren't theirs.

**Nadia (14:26):** Three of those here too.

**Sophie (14:26):** Four.

**Raj (14:27):** So thirteen files, no owner. I'll take that to Diane. Slotting them into a branch advisor queue is the wrong answer — the advisor has no history with the client so it gets worked last by definition.

**Marcus (14:29):** Last thing from me, and it's a soft one. Since the attrition scores showed up, my advisors have started skipping the proactive call entirely on anything scored Low. That's not what the score is for. I have a Low-scored client with a three hundred thousand dollar deposit balance who hasn't had a call. If we let the model decide who gets a relationship, we'll lose someone expensive and be genuinely surprised.

**Raj (14:31):** Noted, and I agree — the score should change the order of the queue, not the length of it. I'll get that written into the advisor guidance rather than leaving it as folklore.
